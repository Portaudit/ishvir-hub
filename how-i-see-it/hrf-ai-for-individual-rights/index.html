HRF AI for Individual Rights: What It Is and What It Has Delivered

By Ishvir Jackpersad · As of 24 November 2026 · How I build

TLDRHRF runs an AI for Individual Rights program that funds open-source, privacy-focused AI tools for dissidents. I wanted to know what it has actually delivered, so I sorted the public record into two piles: things with dates, and things that are only described. Dated: three grant announcements (24 grants, no amounts disclosed), three hackathons, one summit, a toolkit and a newsletter. Described: the Freedom Cluster, a hardware funding program and Agent Camp, with no dates or amounts. I found no outcome data, so I can't say how well any of it works.

This guide was drafted with AI assistance from HRF's own pages and press releases, as of 24 November 2026. I first read the pages on 22 November and checked the program page, the news listing and the grant announcements again on 24 November. Where HRF describes itself, the text says so. I do not verify HRF's claims independently.

The scenario: who controls the AI an activist relies on

Most people use AI through a hosted service. This is general background, not an HRF claim: the provider's servers receive the prompts, so the provider, or anyone with legal power over it, may be able to obtain them. For someone working under a repressive government, that is a risk worth thinking about.

HRF's program page1 frames the issue in two parts. One is documenting how authoritarian governments use AI for surveillance, censorship and propaganda. The other is supporting open-source, privacy-protecting AI that dissidents can run themselves. Running a model locally, or through a private service, is the common thread in the tools it supports.

This guide describes that program. It does not assess the policy arguments around it, which are contested and are HRF's to make.

What the program is

HRF launched hrf.org/ai on 14 May 20252 as the homepage for its AI for Individual Rights initiatives. The same press release says HRF had brought on Craig Vachon to direct the program, and announced an AI track at the Oslo Freedom Forum and a monthly newsletter. The current program page3 does not name a director, so check who leads it now.

HRF describes itself as a U.S.-based 501(c)(3) nonprofit4 and as a nonpartisan organization focused on closed societies5. It calls the program "the world's first" of its kind3 and fully independent6. Those are HRF's descriptions of itself, and the pages give no outside evidence for them.

What is described but not yet shown

The program page3 lists several initiatives that I could not match to a dated announcement, an amount or a count:

  • Freedom Cluster. HRF says it is building its own local inference so activists can run compute away from state surveillance. The page describes it as being built, not launched.
  • Local Intelligence Program. It funds hardware so NGOs and defenders can run AI locally. No amounts, recipients or dates are given.
  • Agent Camp. Multi-day workshops on deploying open-source AI agents. A 15 June 2026 newsletter13 describes launching it, with no dates or attendance.
  • Annual Summit. The page says the summit is annual. I found one dated edition, in San Francisco in November 2025, and no date for a second.
  • Research fellowships. The page mentions fellowships and partnerships on AI-enabled repression, with no names or numbers.

None of this means the work is not happening. It means the public pages do not show it yet, so ask HRF before citing any of it as delivered.

The toolkit and the projects it funds

The toolkit3 is guidance on using AI securely. It recommends privacy-focused chat services, local models, open-source coding agents, personal agents, VPNs and browsers, with examples including Maple AI, Ollama, OpenCode, OpenClaw and Signal. These are third-party tools that HRF recommends, not HRF products. The page cautions that agents are "not privacy tools" on their own.

HRF announced 8, 10 and 6 grants, which is 24 in all by my count (HRF gives no total). Maple AI appears in two of the announcements, so 23 projects are named. HRF's descriptions, shortened:

Questions to ask before relying on it

  1. Who leads the program now? The 2025 press release named a director and the current page does not.
  2. What do the grants look like? Amounts, terms and the application route are not on the announcements. Application details sit on a separate page4.
  3. Where is the Freedom Cluster? Ask whether it is live, who can use it, and what it logs.
  4. What does the Local Intelligence Program fund? Ask for the hardware, the eligibility and the support offered.
  5. When are the next summit, Agent Camp and hackathon? The pages give no dates.
  6. How should the toolkit's tools be judged? HRF lists them as recommendations, and warns that agents are not privacy tools. Check each tool's own documentation before relying on it.
  7. How does HRF measure results? The pages report activity. Ask what changed for the people the tools were built for.
